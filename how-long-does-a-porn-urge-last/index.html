How long does a porn urge last?

Short answer: the peak usually passes in minutes — often 10 to 20 if you don’t feed it. And here’s the honest part up front: no study has ever timed a porn urge exactly. Any page that gives you one precise number is guessing. What we do know, from craving research, is how urges behave — and what makes them pass faster or drag on.

The wave: rise, peak, fall

An urge isn’t a flat wall. It’s a wave. It rises, peaks, and falls — as long as you don’t feed it. That’s the whole idea behind urge surfing: notice where the urge sits in your body, watch it rise and fall, and don’t act on it or fight it.

In the original study, college smokers were taught to ride out their urges in one short session. Afterwards they smoked less, even though the urges themselves didn’t get any weaker (Bowen & Marlatt 2009). Read that again: the urge stayed the same size. What changed was whether they obeyed it. So “how long does it last” is almost the wrong question. The question that matters is whether you act while it’s loud.

What makes it last longer

Feeding it. Looking, even “just a peek,” keeps the wave going. Every glance is another rise.

Arguing with it. Trying not to think about something tends to bring it back more often (Wegner et al. 1987). Dwelling on the urge and talking yourself into it is what feeds craving (Allen et al. 2017).

Staying where it started. Same room, same device, same position. Nothing has changed, so nothing about the urge changes either. Late at night in bed with your phone is the classic version of this.

The shame spiral. “I’m already thinking about it, so I’ve already failed.” That thought doesn’t shorten anything — it sets up the next urge. Shame and moral conflict made people feel more addicted, separately from how much they actually watched (Grubbs et al. 2019).

What makes it pass faster

Leave the room. Close the tab, put the phone face down, and go somewhere with light or other people. Cutting off easy access is the first move in every program that works.

Move hard for two minutes. Twenty pushups or a brisk walk. Short bursts of activity cut cravings sharply (Haasova et al. 2013), and a 15 minute brisk walk held them off for close to an hour (Taylor & Katomeri 2007). Most of this research is on smoking cravings, but the effect on cravings is the point.

Breathe out longer than you breathe in. In for four, out for six. Exhale-focused breathing improved mood and lowered physical arousal (Balban et al. 2023).

Give your eyes a job. A few minutes of a game that ties up your visual imagination, like Tetris, reduced everyday cravings by around 14 points out of 100 (Skorka-Brown et al. 2015). Scrolling doesn’t work the same way — it hands your imagination right back to the urge.

Decide you’ll wait ten minutes. Not forever. Ten minutes. Set a timer and decide again when it goes off. By then the peak has usually passed, and the decision is a different one. If it’s still loud, work the full seven steps instead of white-knuckling it.

Do urges get weaker over time?

Usually, yes — they get less frequent, and each one gets easier to ride out, because you’ve got proof that it passes. But an occasional urge months later is normal, not a failure. What changes most isn’t the size of the wave. It’s whether you obey it.

If they’re constant, getting stronger, or coming with real distress, that’s the point where a tool stops being enough. A therapist who does CBT or ACT has the strongest evidence — across 20 studies and 2,021 people, therapy cut problem porn use by a large amount (López-Pinar et al. 2025). Common questions

Why do some pages say 30 seconds and others say 15 minutes?

Because nobody has timed porn urges in a study, so every number is borrowed from other cravings or made up. The peak of a craving usually passes in minutes, and the honest range people report is about 10 to 20 minutes if you don’t feed it.

What if the urge doesn’t go down?

That’s okay. In the original urge-surfing study, people who rode out urges acted on them less even though the urges didn’t get weaker. The goal is not acting, not feeling nothing. If it’s very strong, move or distract yourself first, then surf what’s left.

Is feeling an urge a relapse?

No. An urge is a feeling, not an action. Getting through one is a success, not a near-miss.

Do urges ever stop completely?

They usually get less frequent and easier to ride out over time, but an occasional urge months later is normal, not a failure. What changes is whether you obey it.
