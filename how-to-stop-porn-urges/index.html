How to stop porn urges when they hit

You’re probably reading this because one just hit, or because they keep hitting and you’re tired of losing. Either way, here’s the short version: you don’t have to win by gritting your teeth. You have to get to the other side of it. These seven steps are how.

Feeling it right now? Skip the reading. A guided 10 to 15 minute session walks you through every step below.

First, stop trying not to think about it

The instinct is to shove the thought away. That backfires. In a classic set of experiments, people told not to think about something ended up thinking about it more often (Wegner et al. 1987). So don’t argue with the urge or try to make it vanish. Redirect it. Every step below gives your body or mind something else to do.

1. Get away from where you are

Close the tab, put the phone face down, and leave the room. Somewhere with light or other people is best. Stepping away from the trigger and cutting off easy access are core moves in the therapy programs that have been tested on problem porn use (Hallberg et al. 2019; Bőthe et al. 2021). If the urge feels strong, turn on a content blocker or Screen Time limit before you do anything else.

2. Move hard for a couple of minutes

Drop and do 20 pushups, or squats if pushups aren’t your thing. Pooling more than 15 trials, short bursts of activity cut cravings sharply, and harder effort worked best (Haasova et al. 2013). For a longer round, steady moderate effort is the sweet spot (Haasova et al. 2014). A 15 minute brisk walk cut cravings and held them off for close to an hour (Taylor & Katomeri 2007). Most of this research is on smoking, but the effect on cravings is the point.

3. Give your eyes and imagination a job

Urges are built out of vivid mental pictures, and tasks that tie up your visual imagination leave less room for them (Kavanagh et al. 2005). That’s why a few minutes of Tetris reduced everyday cravings, including cravings for sex, by around 14 points out of 100 (Skorka-Brown et al. 2015). Any game where you stack, sort or aim works on the same idea. Scrolling doesn’t, because it hands your imagination right back to the urge.

4. Breathe out longer than you breathe in

Try in for four, out for six. Exhale-focused breathing improved mood and lowered physical arousal in a controlled study (Balban et al. 2023). It’s also a decent way to buy yourself the first minute.

5. Think past the next ten minutes

Picture how you’ll actually feel an hour after giving in. Not the moment, the hour after. Thinking about the long-term downsides cut cravings for cigarettes and food by about a third and calmed the brain’s reward response (Kober et al. 2010).

6. Bring to mind what matters to you

Who you want to be, the person you don’t want to let down, the thing you’re building. After a porn craving was set off in 711 people, briefly thinking about something meaningful lowered it more than willpower, mindfulness or awe (Todorovic et al. 2023).

7. Make an if-then plan for next time

While your head is clear, decide: “If I’m in bed with my phone and bored, I’ll get up and do 20 pushups.” Across 94 tests, deciding in advance what you’ll do when a situation shows up made people much more likely to follow through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006).

When the urge is really strong

At high intensity, distraction beats reflection, because thinking things through needs mental bandwidth a strong emotion uses up (Sheppes et al. 2011, 2014). So if it’s an 8 out of 10, start with steps 1 to 3 and save the thinking for when it drops.

Learn what sets yours off

Using porn to cope with stress, boredom or bad feelings is tied to problem use (Bőthe et al. 2021). In a 10 week diary study, binges followed low mood, stress and anxiety, and every man’s triggers were different (Wordecha et al. 2018). Next time, notice what was going on right before. That’s the thing your plan in step 7 should target.

If it keeps happening

That’s not a character flaw, and help works. Across 20 studies and 2,021 people, therapy cut problem use and how often people watched by a large amount (López-Pinar et al. 2025). One form of therapy, ACT, cut viewing by 93% in a trial (Crosby & Twohig 2016). If you can, find a therapist who does CBT or ACT.

An honest note: each of these techniques has research behind it, but a lot of the craving research is on smoking, alcohol and food. The mechanisms likely carry over. Treat the exact numbers as estimates.

Common questions

What’s the fastest way to stop a porn urge?

Get away from the screen and move hard right away. Leave the room and do 20 pushups or squats. Short, hard bursts of activity cut cravings sharply, and getting away from the trigger cuts off easy access.

Should I try to stop thinking about it?

No. Trying not to think about something tends to bring it back more often. Give your mind something else to do instead, like a demanding game, slow breathing or picturing how you’ll feel an hour from now.

Does exercise help with porn urges?

Yes, it’s one of the best-supported options. In studies of cravings, short bursts of activity cut cravings sharply, and a 15 minute brisk walk held them off for close to an hour. Most of this research is on smoking cravings.

When should I get professional help?

If urges feel compulsive, keep winning or are hurting your life, a therapist who does CBT or ACT has the strongest evidence. Across 20 studies, therapy cut problem porn use by a large amount.
