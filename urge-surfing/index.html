Urge surfing: how to ride out a porn urge

Urge surfing sounds a little soft. It isn’t. It’s a way of treating an urge like a wave: you don’t fight it and you don’t obey it. You stay on top of it while it rises, peaks and falls. It comes from addiction research, and it works on a surprisingly simple idea.

The idea: you don’t have to make the urge go away

In the original study, college smokers were taught to ride out their urges in one short session. Afterwards they smoked less, even though the urges themselves didn’t get any weaker (Bowen & Marlatt 2009). Read that again. The urge stayed the same size. What changed was whether they obeyed it.

That takes a lot of pressure off. You’re not trying to feel nothing. You’re trying to feel it and not act.

Why fighting it makes it worse

When you try to push a thought away, it tends to come back more often (Wegner et al. 1987). An urge you argue with gets bigger. An urge you watch gets boring.

How to urge surf, step by step

1. Stop and name it. Say to yourself, “This is an urge.” Naming a feeling reduced the brain’s alarm response to emotional images in brain-scan research (Lieberman et al. 2007). Give it a number from 0 to 10, too.

2. Find it in your body. Where is it? Chest, stomach, hands, face? Is it hot, tight, buzzing? Describe it like you’re explaining it to someone else. You’re turning a command into a set of sensations.

3. Breathe and stay with it. In for four, out for six. Exhale-focused breathing lowers physical arousal (Balban et al. 2023). You don’t need to slow the urge down. Just keep breathing next to it.

4. Catch the thoughts it sends. Urges come with excuses. “Just this once.” “You deserve it.” “You already blew today.” Dwelling on the urge and talking yourself into it is what feeds craving (Allen et al. 2017). Label each one: “That’s the urge talking.” You don’t have to answer it.

5. Watch it rise, peak and fall. Notice when it gets stronger. Notice when it starts to ease. Check the number again. Every time you ride one out without acting on it, you get proof that you can.

Does it work for porn?

The early evidence says yes. In a pilot study, thirteen men with compulsive sexual behavior spent much less time on porn after eight weeks of mindfulness-based relapse prevention, the program urge surfing is part of (Holas et al. 2020). In a larger trial of 286 people, that same program cut drug use and heavy drinking over a full year (Bowen et al. 2014).

A closely related therapy, ACT, teaches the same skill of noticing urges and thoughts without obeying them. In a trial on problem internet porn use, it cut viewing by 93%, compared with 21% for people on a waitlist (Crosby & Twohig 2016).

When surfing isn’t enough

If the urge is an 8 or 9 out of 10, sitting still with it can be too much. At high intensity, distraction works better than reflection (Sheppes et al. 2011). So move first. Do 20 pushups, which cut cravings sharply (Haasova et al. 2013), or play a demanding game. Then surf what’s left once it drops a few points. That order, distract when it’s loud and surf when it’s quieter, is how the Urge Helper session is built.

A few things people get wrong

It’s not about relaxing. You can surf an urge while tense. It’s not about clearing your mind either. Thoughts will keep coming, and that’s fine. And it’s not a failure if the urge comes back ten minutes later. Surf it again. Most people find each wave a little easier than the one before, because they’ve got proof that it passes.

An honest note: most urge-surfing research is on smoking, drinking and drugs. The porn-specific studies are small but promising. It’s a skill, so it gets easier the more you practise it.

Common questions

What is urge surfing?

Urge surfing is a mindfulness skill where you notice an urge, describe what it feels like in your body, and watch it rise and fall without acting on it or fighting it. It comes from addiction research.

Does urge surfing work for porn?

Early evidence says yes. In a small pilot study, men with compulsive sexual behavior spent much less time on porn after eight weeks of mindfulness-based relapse prevention, which includes urge surfing. A related therapy, ACT, cut porn viewing by 93% in a trial.

What if the urge doesn’t go down?

That’s okay. In the original study, people who rode out urges acted on them less, even though the urges didn’t get weaker. The goal is not acting, not feeling nothing. If it’s very strong, move or distract yourself first, then surf.

Is urge surfing the same as distraction?

No. Distraction moves your attention away from the urge. Urge surfing turns toward it and watches it. Distraction tends to work better when an urge is very strong, and surfing when it’s milder, so they work well together.
